Strategy Theory Notes
Companion to Model Evaluation Summary.txt. Covers every architectural building
block and every exposure-control mechanic used across Sigma7p, Sigma8, SigmaB10,
Sigma10X, Sigma10FB, Sigma16, and Sigma17, with the underlying theory each one
is drawing on.

======================================================================
PART ONE: THE STRATEGY BUILDING BLOCKS
======================================================================

A) SEED PAIRS: COINTEGRATED PAIR TRADING
----------------------------------------
Present in: every model.

The core idea. Two instruments are cointegrated if some linear combination of
their (log) prices is stationary, even though each price series individually
is a non-stationary random walk. Concretely, if log(P_i) and log(P_j) are each
integrated of order 1 (a random walk has no fixed mean, its variance grows
without bound), but a spread S = log(P_i) - beta*log(P_j) reverts to a fixed
mean, then i and j are cointegrated with cointegrating vector (1, -beta). This
is the Engle-Granger framework (Engle & Granger, 1987): estimate beta by
regressing one price on the other, then test the *residual* for stationarity.

How the code tests this. The `ar1_t` function fits an AR(1) model to the
(demeaned) spread: spread_t = phi * spread_{t-1} + noise, and converts phi into
a t-statistic against the null phi = 1 (a unit root, i.e. a random walk, i.e.
NOT mean-reverting). This is a simplified Dickey-Fuller test. A strongly
negative t-stat (the code's gates sit around -2.5 to -3.4) rejects the unit-root
null with confidence, meaning the spread is behaving like a mean-reverting
process rather than a random walk.

The generating process underneath is best thought of as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck
(OU) process: dS = -kappa*(S - mu)*dt + sigma*dW. kappa controls the speed of
reversion, and the half-life of any deviation is ln(2)/kappa. A higher |phi-1|
(more negative t-stat) corresponds to a larger kappa, faster reversion, a
shorter half-life. The project's own measurements put the seed pairs' half-life
around a week, which is why RESCAN=20 (looking for new pairs every 20 days) and
WIN=450 (the window used to *estimate* the hedge ratio and mean) are sensible:
you want your estimation window long enough for a stable beta, but your trading
signal reactive on a timescale close to the half-life.

Trading rule. Once a pair is confirmed, the model computes a z-score of the
current spread against its own rolling mean and (EWMA-smoothed) volatility, and
takes a bang-bang position (full leg size, or flat) whenever |z| exceeds a
band (BAND=0.25 or so). This is classic statistical arbitrage: you are long the
"cheap" leg and short the "expensive" leg in the cointegrating ratio, betting
the spread reverts, and you are close to market-neutral *within the pair*
because both legs move together with the common factor (this is why the
`beta` in the regression matters: it is precisely the hedge ratio that cancels
the shared component between the two names, leaving only the idiosyncratic
spread to bet on).

The two-window (KEEPR / T_KEEP vs the full-history T_KEEP) gating you see
everywhere (e.g. `if tstat[0]<T_KEEP and rec_t(spread[0])<KEEPR`) is a
robustness check on top of Engle-Granger: it's not enough for a spread to have
looked stationary over the full sample, it must ALSO look stationary
*recently*. This guards against a classic pairs-trading failure mode: a
relationship that cointegrated historically but has since broken down (a
"regime change" in the cointegrating relationship itself), which would make you
keep betting on convergence that no longer happens.

B) TWO-FACTOR LEAD-LAG MATRIX
------------------------------
Present in: every model.

The idea. Beyond pairs, there may be a broader (but individually weak) linear
predictive structure: today's move in name i tells you something about
tomorrow's move in name j. The code estimates this directly as a dense n x n
matrix L where L[i,j] = correlation(residual_i(t), residual_j(t+1)) using all
history, i.e. a full one-lag cross-correlation matrix. This is conceptually a
restricted Vector Autoregression, VAR(1): x_{t+1} = L^T * x_t + noise, except
here L is estimated column-by-column via simple dot products rather than a
joint multivariate regression, and it is never fit with any variable selection
(the project's own notes found LASSO feature-selected lead-lag showed a small
in-sample edge but did not survive out-of-sample re-testing, so the dense
matrix was kept).

Why "residual" and why "two-factor". Before computing L, returns are
residualized against ALGO (the market factor, since ALGO correlates ~0.99 with
the equal-weight basket) and then against the first principal component of
what's left (`NFAC=2`). This is the same logic as the Arbitrage Pricing Theory
(APT, Ross 1976) or a Fama-French-style factor model: you don't want your
cross-sectional forecast to just be rediscovering "the whole market moved
together yesterday, so it'll keep moving together" (systematic co-movement).
Stripping the known factor (ALGO) and the largest remaining latent factor (via
PCA, i.e. the eigenvector of the residual covariance matrix with the largest
eigenvalue) isolates the *idiosyncratic* lead-lag structure, which is a much
more specific, and more likely genuinely local, signal.

Why this works at all with a dense, unfiltered matrix. Any single entry
L[i,j] is a noisy correlation over ~50-450 observations and, taken alone, is
close to statistically insignificant. But the *aggregate* forecast for name j
sums 49 such terms: `raw_ll_signal = zres[:,-1] @ L`. This is a breadth
argument, formalized in quantitative finance as Grinold's Fundamental Law of
Active Management: expected performance scales with Information Coefficient
(the average per-signal correlation with the outcome) times the square root of
breadth (the number of roughly independent bets). Many individually weak,
roughly-independent correlations can aggregate into a much stronger combined
forecast, which is exactly why the dense, "everything vs everything" matrix
beats sparser, seemingly cleaner alternatives (top-K sparsification, shrinkage,
ridge) in this project's own testing: those techniques all reduce breadth
without a compensating improvement in per-entry quality.

The "recency blend" refinement (SigmaB10 onward: LL_ROLL=450, LL_BLEND=0.1)
blends 90% of the full-history matrix with 10% of a matrix estimated only on
the trailing 450 days. This is a bias-variance tradeoff: the full-history
matrix has lower variance (more data) but can be stale if the underlying
lead-lag relationships drift; the recent matrix is higher variance but more
current. A small blend weight is a cheap way to let the estimate track drift
without giving up most of the statistical power of the long history.

C) GRAY / SURVIVOR TIER
------------------------
Present in: Sigma8 onward.

Beyond the 7 hand-verified seed pairs, there is a much larger population of
candidate pairs that pass a *looser* cointegration gate (full-history t<-3.4,
recent t<-2.8 in this project's convention, versus a stricter -4.0-ish gate for
what the notes call "strict survivor" pairs). Individually, many of these are
almost certainly false positives: with 50 names there are 50*49/2 = 1225
possible pairs, and even under a pure-noise null, a meaningful fraction will
pass a t<-2.6-ish screen by chance alone (this is a multiple-comparisons
problem, the same phenomenon that makes "data snooping" dangerous in any large
factor search).

The mitigation is not to raise the bar until only "true" pairs remain (the
project's own capacity sweep found doing so is strictly worse), but to trade
the whole tier at *half weight* (GW=0.5) alongside the full-weight measured
core. This is again the breadth argument from section B, applied to portfolio
construction rather than forecasting: a large, diversified set of small,
positively-biased-but-individually-noisy bets can have a better risk-adjusted
return than a small set of "cleaner" bets, provided the average edge per bet is
still positive and the bets are not all correlated with each other. Down-
weighting (rather than fully trusting) the gray tier is a simple way to size
for the fact that its average per-unit edge is lower and its false-positive
rate is higher than the seed tier's.

D) BIRTH-CATCHER (RECENT-WINDOW DETECTOR)
-------------------------------------------
Present in: Sigma8 onward, gate loosened from -4.0 to -3.5 in Sigma10FB/16/17.

This detector looks for pairs that are cointegrated on a *recent* window
(BIRTHW=150 days) but were NOT cointegrated (or only weakly so, `full_t>-2.6`)
over the full history. In other words: it is explicitly looking for
relationships that did not exist before and have since appeared.

Why this matters here specifically: the project's own measurement of the price
generator found that the pair network rewires substantially over time (~73%
edge turnover between one 250-day window and the next, even though the total
pair count stays roughly stable). This means the population of tradeable
cointegrated pairs is not a fixed, discoverable-once set; it is closer to a
non-stationary, regime-switching graph where old edges die and new edges are
born. A static "find pairs once on day 1 and trade them forever" strategy will
progressively lose coverage as its discovered edges decay. The birth-catcher is
a structural-break detector aimed squarely at this: it is the mechanism that
lets the book adapt to newly-formed relationships without needing to re-run a
full historical scan (which the gray tier already does, but more slowly and
without specifically weighting for recency).

The loosening of BGATE from -4.0 to -3.5 (first appearing alongside Sigma10FB)
is a straightforward capacity increase on this same detector: a less negative
t-stat threshold admits more candidate "births," at the cost of a higher false-
positive rate among them. The project's own neighbourhood sweep of this
parameter (from -4.0 down through -3.2 in 0.1 steps) found a broad plateau from
about -3.7 to -3.3, which is the textbook signature of a real, robust effect
rather than a lucky single value; -3.5, sitting in the middle of that plateau,
is the one parameter change from that whole sweep that was judged to
generalize.

E) TAIL-REGIME DIAGNOSTICS AND HYSTERESIS (STICKY VOTES)
-----------------------------------------------------------
Present in: SigmaB10 onward (via `_pair_vote` / `_ll_response` and the
`pair_tail_weak` / `ll_tail_mode` diagnostics).

The default assumption for both the pair spreads and the lead-lag signal is
that the relationship between "how far the signal is from zero" and "what
happens next" is roughly LINEAR and uniform across the whole range: the
further the z-score, the more you want to bet, all the way out. But this need
not hold at the extremes. The `_update_regimes` function explicitly tests for
this by fitting two separate regression slopes: one using only observations
near the centre of the distribution (|z| < 1.5) and one using only observations
in the tail (|z| >= 2.0), each against the realized next-period response. If
the tail slope is *reliably weaker* than the centre slope (statistically,
`pt > pc + 2*(pce+pte)`, i.e. the tail's reversion is worse than the centre's
by more than twice the combined standard error), the diagnostic concludes the
tail is unreliable: extreme readings do not revert the way moderate ones do.

This is a meaningful, non-obvious idea worth connecting to a few different
literatures at once:
  - It is related to the empirical finding that mean reversion in real markets
    is often regime-dependent: moderate deviations tend to revert, but very
    large deviations can instead signal a genuine structural break, an
    information event, or the start of a trend (i.e. momentum can dominate
    reversal exactly in the tail, the opposite of what a naive linear model
    assumes).
  - It is also the finance-native analogue of "leptokurtosis" (fat tails):
    linear reversion models implicitly assume something like a Gaussian,
    constant-kappa OU process, but real (and apparently this generator's)
    spreads can behave differently once you're several standard deviations
    out.
  - The remedy the code applies, once tail-weakness is detected, is a
    hysteresis rule (see `_pair_vote`): rather than a flat "in or out" band,
    the position now has memory. A new position only opens once |z| clears the
    entry band; once open, it is held (not flipped to flat) unless the
    magnitude actually collapses back toward zero (PAIR_EXIT_Z) or blows out to
    a hard stop (PAIR_STOP_Z=3.0), at which point it is cut regardless of
    direction. This is precisely a Schmitt-trigger / dead-band pattern from
    control theory (a system that only changes state once a threshold is
    crossed by a meaningful margin, to avoid "chattering" on noise near a
    boundary), applied here as something closer to a stop-loss discipline:
    don't fight a move that has already invalidated your reversion
    assumption, and don't get shaken out of a good position by noise near the
    entry line either.

======================================================================
PART TWO: THE EXPOSURE-CONTROL MECHANICS (THE "ALGO SLOT")
======================================================================

Background common to all five mechanics below. ALGO (instrument 0) is treated
throughout as the market factor: it has a 10x larger position limit ($100k vs
$10k) and a 5x lower commission rate (0.02% vs 0.1%), so whatever occupies this
slot has an outsized effect on total book PnL and Sharpe. Every model computes
`hedge_dollars`, the dollar amount of ALGO that would exactly cancel the rest
of the book's *incidental*, unintentional exposure to the market factor (its
"beta"), via `hedge_dollars = -sum(target_i * price_i * beta_i)`, where beta_i
is each name's own regression coefficient on ALGO's returns. This single
computed quantity is then used differently by each mechanic below.

1) PURE BETA HEDGE (Sigma7p, Sigma8)
--------------------------------------
`target[0] = hedge_dollars / price[0]`, full stop.

This is the direct implementation of market-neutral portfolio construction, a
concept going back to the CAPM decomposition of any asset's return into a
systematic (beta * market return) and an idiosyncratic (alpha) component. If
your pairs and lead-lag book, by the accident of which names it happens to be
long or short on a given day, ends up net long or short the market factor, that
exposure is NOT something the strategy has any forecasting edge on; it is pure,
uncompensated risk that only adds variance without adding expected return. Fully
cancelling it with an offsetting ALGO position is the textbook way to isolate
"pure alpha": you keep the return from the stat-arb/lead-lag edges, and you
discard the return (and, more importantly here, the extra variance) that comes
along for the ride purely because of position construction. This is why Sigma7p
and Sigma8 are described as "beta-neutral, no directional bet": the ALGO slot
here is doing risk management, not forecasting.

2) DIRECTIONAL REVERSAL BET (SigmaB10, Sigma16, and half of Sigma17)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
`target[0] = ALGO_REV_DOLLARS * sign(-(log(P0,t) - log(P0,t-5)))`, i.e. fade
ALGO's own trailing 5-day log return, at the full $100k cap, hedge turned off.

This abandons the market-neutral framing entirely and instead treats ALGO
itself as a forecastable instrument: bet that whatever ALGO has just done over
5 days, it will partially reverse. This is the short-term reversal effect
documented in the academic literature (e.g. Lehmann 1990, Jegadeesh 1990):
at short (weekly-to-monthly) horizons, returns tend to exhibit negative serial
correlation, commonly attributed to short-term overreaction, inventory effects
from market-making/liquidity provision, or bid-ask bounce. It is the mirror
image of the (much better known) momentum effect at 3-12 month horizons
(Jegadeesh & Titman 1993), where returns are positively autocorrelated; reversal
and momentum are not contradictory, they are different phenomena that dominate
at different horizons.

The critical caveat, directly measured in this project rather than assumed: the
project measured ALGO's own lag-1 autocorrelation on one window at -0.094
(genuinely mean-reverting, and the reversal signal was profitable at every
horizon 1-15 days tested), and then on the next window at +0.042 (the sign
flipped: ALGO was now trending, not reverting, and the same reversal signal
lost money at every horizon tested). This is an important, general lesson about
"anomalies" like short-term reversal: they are empirical regularities of a
particular market regime, not physical laws, and they can and do invert. A
strategy built on one is making an implicit, time-varying bet on which regime
currently holds, whether or not that is stated explicitly.

3) ANTI-HEDGE / LEVERAGE (Sigma10X)
--------------------------------------
`target[0] = -1.5 * hedge_dollars / price[0]` (note the sign: since
`hedge_dollars` already points opposite to the book's incidental exposure,
multiplying it by a NEGATIVE weight flips it back to point WITH that exposure,
amplified 1.5x).

This is the most theoretically interesting of the five mechanics, precisely
because it looks superficially like "leverage" (a standard, often-value-
additive tool: scale up a position that has positive expected value) but is
built on a fundamentally different, and here empirically falsified,
assumption. Ordinary leverage on a genuine risk factor (say, market beta in a
CAPM sense) is defensible because the market risk premium is believed to be
persistently positive over time; you're scaling up exposure to something with
positive expected return, accepting more variance for more expected return
(this is the basic logic behind risk parity and leveraged-beta strategies).

But the book's "incidental exposure" here is not a risk factor with a
persistent premium; it is simply whatever residual market-tilt happens to fall
out of running a market-neutral stat-arb book on a given day, a byproduct of
position construction with no design intent behind its sign. The project
directly measured its correlation with ALGO's next-day return at -0.071 on one
window and +0.106 on the other (sign flips between windows), and its rolling
50-day block PnL alternates sign more often than chance would even predict
(consecutive-same-sign only 4 of 10 transitions, versus roughly 50% expected;
lag-1 autocorrelation of block PnL measured at -0.30, i.e. mildly anti-
persistent). This is the statistical signature of pure noise, not a risk
premium: a genuine risk factor's exposure should have a reasonably stable sign
and a reasonably persistent premium over time; something with a coin-flip sign
and no persistence has an expected value of zero, and amplifying it (via
leverage) only multiplies its variance for no expected-return benefit. This is
the same warning underlying the Kelly criterion and any variance-aware position
sizing framework: you should only lever up something with genuine positive
expectancy, and confirming that expectancy (via things like sign persistence
and cross-window stability, exactly as measured here) has to come BEFORE the
leverage decision, not be assumed from a single lucky backtest window (548 on
one window, 806 on the other, in this project's own numbers, with the
two-window average barely different from not touching the ALGO slot at all).

4) FLAT / UNTRADED (Sigma10FB)
---------------------------------
`target[0] = 0` always (both the reversal weight and hedge weight are zero).

The simplest of the five, and arguably the most useful for interpretation
purposes even though it is not a "strategy" in the trading sense at all: it is
a controlled ablation. By fully disabling the ALGO slot, this configuration
isolates exactly what the rest of the book (pairs, gray tier, birth-catcher,
lead-lag) is worth on its own, with no confound from any ALGO-slot decision.
This is standard scientific method applied to strategy research: to know what
a component contributes, hold everything else fixed and remove only that
component, then compare. Every other exposure mechanic in this document should,
in principle, be evaluated relative to this flat baseline, since that is the
cleanest measure of "what did the ALGO slot actually add or subtract."

5) RELATIVE REVERSAL (half of Sigma17's ALGO slot, weight 0.5)
------------------------------------------------------------------
`rel_now = -((log(P0,t) - log(P0,t-6)) - sum_of_mean_non_ALGO_returns_over_6_days)`
then bet on sign(rel_now), blended 50/50 with mechanic 2 above (the plain
5-day reversal).

Where mechanic 2 fades ALGO's own move in isolation, this fades ALGO's move
*relative to* the equal-weight basket of everything else: it asks not "did
ALGO go up or down" but "did ALGO go up or down MORE than the rest of the
market did." This is structurally a relative-value or spread trade (the same
family of idea as the seed pairs in Part One, section A, just with one leg
being a single name, ALGO, and the other leg being an equal-weighted basket of
49 names instead of a single partner instrument), sometimes called dispersion
or relative-strength trading when applied to an index versus its constituents.

The genuinely important nuance, again directly investigated rather than
assumed in this project: a correctly-lagged decomposition of this signal's
realized PnL found that the "spread" component (the actual relative-value
convergence between ALGO and the basket) contributed close to zero, sometimes
slightly negative. Almost all of the measured edge (roughly +11bp/day) came
from the BASKET's own next-day return, using ALGO's relative underperformance
merely as a trigger/timing variable. In other words, this is not really a
relative-value arbitrage trade at all; it is a market-factor-timing signal (a
forecast of the basket's own next-day direction) that happens to use a
relative-value-shaped construction to generate its trigger. That distinction
matters a great deal for how much to trust it going forward: it is the same
broad category of bet as mechanic 3 (anti-hedge), which was shown to be a
zero-mean coinflip, and this one has not been proven immune to the same risk,
it has simply not (yet, as of the last two available windows) exhibited the
same sign-flipping instability. The project's own notes flag this explicitly
as a risk carried forward, not a settled question.

======================================================================
HOW TO READ THIS AGAINST MODEL EVALUATION SUMMARY.TXT
======================================================================
Each named model is just a specific combination of: which of Part One's
building blocks are switched on (and at what gate/weight), plus exactly one of
Part Two's five exposure mechanics (or, for Sigma17, a blend of two of them) in
the ALGO slot. Reading a model's Architecture and Exposure bullets alongside
this document should tell you not just WHAT each model does differently, but
WHY that difference is expected (or, in several cases, empirically shown NOT)
to matter.
